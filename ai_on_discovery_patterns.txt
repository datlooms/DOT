### 1. Structural Framing: Beyond Greedy Sets and Individual Ranks

You are currently trying to select *elements* (signals) to maximize an emergent property of *sets* (concurrency). This is fundamentally a hypergraph problem, not a vector-ranking or graph-clustering problem.

A single triple is a hyperedge connecting three feature states. A bar with depth $N$ is a node where $N$ hyperedges intersect. You do not care about the properties of any individual hyperedge; you care about the **topology of their intersections**.

Here are three mathematical frameworks better suited to this structure:

#### A. Bipartite Spectral Decomposition (Bar-Signal Matrix)
Construct a binary incidence matrix $M$ of size $\text{Bars} \times \text{Candidates}$ (where $M_{i,j} = 1$ if signal $j$ fires on bar $i$). 

* **The Fallacy of Jaccard/Overlap:** Standard correlation measures $M^T M$ (signal-to-signal overlap). Maximizing off-diagonal elements gives redundancy; minimizing them gives disjoint signals.
* **The Spectral Alternative:** Run a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) on $M$ or analyze the spectrum of the Laplacian $L = M M^T$ (bar-to-bar similarity through shared signals). You are looking for a **low-rank subspace** of signals that consistently co-occur to form high-density clusters in bar space, rather than individual orthogonal signals. The singular vectors of $M$ will reveal "modules" or "facets" of market state that fire collectively.

#### B. Submodular Set Cover with Satiation
Your performance curve is non-linear: depth 1–2 is noise, depth 3–5 is acceptable, depth $>15$ is high-conviction. standard submodular optimization (like Max-Coverage) exhibits diminishing marginal returns, whereas your payoff function has **increasing returns up to a threshold $N$, and diminishing returns thereafter**.

This is a **Satiation/Threshold Submodular Function**. Instead of optimizing trade performance directly, define a utility function $F(S)$ over candidate set $S$:
$$F(S) = \sum_{\text{bars } b} g\left(\sum_{s \in S} \mathbf{1}_{s \text{ fires on } b}\right)$$
Where $g(k)$ is a step/logistic function that yields 0 utility for depth $k < 3$, steep positive utility for $3 \le k \le 20$, and flat utility for $k > 20$ (to penalize trivial over-saturation). Selecting $S$ to maximize $F(S)$ subject to a cardinality constraint $|S| \le K$ forces the solver to pick signals that *cooperate* to push high-quality bars past the depth floor, without blowing up the bar count everywhere else.

#### C. Frequent Pattern Mining / Hypergraph Core Decomposition
In transaction database literature, this is the **Frequent Itemset Mining (FIM)** problem with a minimum utility constraint. Instead of searching candidate signals, treat each bar as a "basket" of firing variables. 
* Perform **$k$-core decomposition** on the hypergraph of signals. The $k$-core identifies maximal subhypergraphs where every signal participates in at least $k$ co-occurrences.
* The 297 working signals likely form a cohesive $k$-core in signal-space.

### 2. Extending Null-Model Scoring to Sets

Your "expected count under matched nulls" works because it controls the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) at the candidate level via False Discovery Rate (FDR) principles. 1. Generate $B$ permutation matrices where each column (signal) maintains its exact daily/session firing frequency and autocorrelations, but its timestamps are randomly phase-shifted or permuted via block bootstrap.
2. For any candidate subset $S$, observe the distribution of depth per bar $D_b(S) = \sum_{s \in S} \mathbf{1}_{s \text{ fires on } b}$.

Define the **Set-Level Significance Score** as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence or Higher Criticism statistic between the observed depth distribution $P_{obs}(D(S))$ and the null depth distribution $P_0(D(S))$:

$$\text{Score}(S) = \text{HC}^*(S) = \sup_{k \ge 3} \frac{\text{Observed Bars}(D \ge k) - \text{Expected Bars}_{P_0}(D \ge k)}{\sqrt{\text{Variance}_{P_0}(D \ge k)}}$$

**Why this works:** It mathematically links the selection objective to the statistical impossibility of the co-occurrence. * **Phase Transitions in Random Constraint Satisfaction Problems (K-SAT):** In computer science, 3-SAT problems exhibit a sharp threshold (a phase transition). Below a critical density of constraints, solutions are abundant and uninformative; above it, the solution space collapses abruptly. Your depth floor is effectively an **over-constrained system** where market noise cannot satisfy $N$ simultaneous non-linear constraints, leaving only structural/forced flows (e.g., liquidation cascades, programmatic execution).

* **Coincidence Detection in Neural Decoding:** In neuroscience, cortical neurons act as coincidence detectors. Single synaptic inputs (depth 1) decay as noise; when $N$ inputs arrive within a tight millisecond window, the membrane potential breaches a non-linear threshold and fires an action potential. 18. One statistical idea I would seriously investigate: Model Confidence Set / set-valued output

Instead of selecting one book, you could ask:

Which candidate books are statistically indistinguishable from the best one?

The Model Confidence Set literature explicitly constructs a set containing the best model with a specified confidence level and does not require believing that one model is “the true model.”

For your problem, the analogy would be:

define a family of plausible selection rules;
generate candidate books;
compare them on held-out blocks;
eliminate books that are demonstrably inferior;
retain the confidence set of non-inferior books.

Then you can examine membership frequencies across the surviving books.

A detector appearing in 82% of plausible books is interesting.

One appearing in only one arbitrary optimum is not.

That would give you a principled answer to the instability problem without pretending it isn't there.

19. And I would borrow an idea from post-selection inference

There is a whole statistical literature on the fact that inference after selection is different from ordinary inference. The major approaches include sample splitting, simultaneous inference, and conditional selective inference.

In your setting, exact selective inference is probably impractical.

But the philosophy is important:

The null distribution must know that selection occurred.

That is exactly what your current candidate-level expected-count procedure does partially, and what your proposed set-level extension should do fully.

---

### 1. The missing framings

You asked about submodular, spectral, and information-theoretic approaches. The problem is **supermodular**, not submodular, and that is why greedy selection fails. A signal’s marginal value is near-zero until the set around it is large enough to push bars over the depth floor; after that threshold, the marginal value jumps. That is increasing returns, not diminishing returns. Greedy maximization of a supermodular function has no approximation guarantee and is path-dependent by nature. The 12 % overlap across split halves is not a failure of your heuristic; it is the expected behavior of any local search on a frustrated energy landscape.

Here are three framings that fit the structure you described better than ranking or greedy growth.

**A. Synergistic information (Partial Information Decomposition).**  
Your 297 sit mid-field on every marginal statistic because their information is entirely synergistic: no single triple knows anything, but the joint state of many triples knows a lot. The PID literature (Williams & Beer; later Ince/Lizier) formalizes exactly this: information that exists only in the joint distribution and in no lower-order marginal. Your depth floor is a physical readout of synergy. The selection problem is therefore: find the largest set with high *synergistic* mutual information about the target, under a constraint that the set does not collapse into redundancy (clones). Standard mRMR and mutual-information feature selection are useless here because they optimize relevance minus redundancy; you need relevance *through* a specific, calibrated amount of redundancy.

**B. Planted dense subgraph in a weighted hypergraph.**  
Treat each candidate triple as a vertex. A one-minute bar is a hyperedge whose weight is the realized return. You want a subset of vertices that contains many heavy hyperedges of size ≥ N, but whose pairwise edge density (co-firing) is not so high that the subgraph is just a clique of clones. This is the "planted community" problem: there is a latent community of signals that co-activate on good bars, but the community is soft and overlapping. The reason Jaccard fails in both directions is that you are trying to optimize a single scalar (overlap) when the truth is a two-dimensional constraint: not too little overlap (or you never reach depth), not too much (or you have no diversity). You need a **bipartite expansion** condition, not a density condition.

**C. Error-correcting code / threshold circuit design.**  
Each triple is a parity-check or a local test. The depth floor is a majority-logic decoder. You want a code (the book) such that the decoder rarely fires on noise (low false positive rate at depth 3) but has high recall for the true message (many true positives at depth ≥ 3). The right literature is not feature selection; it is the design of **low-density parity-check codes** or **threshold logic circuits**. The question "which triples to keep" is equivalent to "which parity checks to include so that the belief-propagation decoder has a wide basin of attraction." This sounds exotic, but it captures your empirical finding: the best codes are not the checks with the highest individual reliability; they are the checks whose *dependence structure* aligns with the noise geometry.

---

### 2. From individual significance to set significance

Your per-candidate null is the only stable thing because it is a *within-family* rank. It strips out calendar variation by conditioning on the search space. But it cannot be extended to sets by simply multiplying p-values, because the candidates are dependent (they share raw variables, and they share the same price path).

A principled extension is to define a **set-level null that preserves the observed dependency structure among candidates, not just their marginals.**

Concretely:  
- Fit an **exponential random graph model (ERGM)** or a **latent Dirichlet allocation** model to the 20,000 × 20,000 co-firing matrix, where the sufficient statistics are: each candidate’s firing rate, each pairwise co-firing rate, and (if computable) each triple’s co-firing rate. This is your null graphical model. It encodes "how would 20,000 signals fire if they had the same pairwise dependencies as the real ones but no collective regime-detection ability."  
- From this null, sample sets of size 297. Compute the depth-distribution statistic on each sampled set: e.g., the area under the depth-vs-profit-factor curve, or the fraction of bars at depth ≥ 20.  
- The p-value of your actual set is its percentile in this null distribution.

If you cannot fit an ERGM, use a **sequential permutation null**: hold the 297 signals fixed, but permute the bar labels (returns) in blocks of one day to preserve intraday autocorrelation. If the depth-PF structure survives on permuted returns, your set is just a volatility calendar spread, not a consensus detector.

The deeper point: your current filter asks "is this signal special relative to random triples?" The set-level test asks "is this *constellation* special relative to random constellations with the same wiring diagram?" Those are different null hypotheses, and the second one is the correct standard for generalization.

---

### 3. The depth floor: literature and nature

The depth floor is not ensemble voting in the Breiman sense. It is a **nonparametric regime-switching detector**, and the nonlinear payoff is the signature of a latent state.

**Literature to read:**

- **Regime-switching models:** Hamilton (1989) and the subsequent hidden-Markov literature in econometrics. Your depth count is a proxy for P(regime = predictive | data). The gates you built (strictest at shallow depth) are exactly what a Bayesian would do: when the posterior is weak, you demand more prior confirmation; when the posterior is already overwhelming, you do not.
- **Condorcet jury theorem with correlated jurors:** Ladha (1992), Berg (1993). Your triples are jurors. The theorem says majority accuracy depends on the average pairwise correlation between jurors. Your 1.5× co-firing is that correlation. The theorem predicts a sharp threshold: below a certain correlation, majority accuracy collapses; above it, it approaches unity. Your jump from PF 5 to PF 35 is that threshold.
- **Synergy in neural populations:** Schneidman et al. (2003), Latham & Nirenberg. Neural populations show exactly your pattern: single neurons are weakly informative, but the population state at high activity is highly predictive. The readout mechanism is often a threshold on population activity, just like your depth floor.
- **N-version programming / multi-channel safety systems:** The engineering literature on requiring N independent sensors to agree before triggering an action. The insight there is that the value is not in the sensors but in the *independence assumption* being true.

The conceptual shift: stop thinking of the depth floor as a "filter" applied to signals. Think of it as an **estimator of a latent variable** that causes the signals. The signals do not predict the price; the latent regime predicts both the price and the signals. The depth floor is your sufficient statistic for that latent variable.

---

### 4. Is a single deterministic rule wrong to want?

Yes and no.

**No:** If the problem were convex or submodular, a single criterion would be correct and an ensemble would be a confession of failure.

**Yes:** Your problem is supermodular on a non-convex landscape with many near-degenerate optima. In that setting, an ensemble of selection criteria is not a symptom; it is a **regularization strategy**. You are doing model averaging over selection procedures, which is well-founded (Bayesian model averaging, stacking, etc.).

**But:** The legitimacy depends entirely on whether the four objectives were **pre-specified** or **selected from a larger menu**. If you tried twelve objectives, kept the four that worked, and unioned them, you have meta-overfitted. The ensemble is then just a more sophisticated form of data mining. The test is: can you write down the four objectives and their union rule *before* seeing the next six months of data? If yes, the ensemble is a valid regularizer. If no, it is a higher-dimensional overfit.

My concrete suggestion: treat the ensemble as a **diversification constraint**, not as a selection rule. Require that any acceptable book must contain at least α% of signals from each of four independent selection principles. That turns the union from an ad-hoc blend into a **coverage guarantee** on the solution space.

---

### 5. What to attack first to prove overfitting

You have done the standard things. Here are five attacks that go deeper, ordered by what I think is most likely to kill the system.

**Attack 1: The concurrency cap is hiding a liquidity hallucination.**  
If 45 independent triples agree on a bar, you are not the only one who sees it. The cap decides which trades you take when the fire hose opens. That cap is almost certainly optimized on the same data that produced the depth-floor logic. The overfitting is not in the signals; it is in the **resource allocation rule** that selects among simultaneous opportunities. To test: degrade your fill assumption to the worst price in the bar, or assume only the first *k* signals (ordered by some exogenous rule, e.g., alphabetical by variable name) get filled. If performance is sensitive to tie-breaking, the cap is the overfit.

**Attack 2: The bar boundary is the signal.**  
Your entire structure depends on events aligning within the same 1-minute bar. If the true latent event is 90 seconds wide, a 1-minute bar splits it; if it is 30 seconds, a 1-minute bar dilutes it with noise. The depth floor may be an artifact of the bar size, not of the market. Test: shift your bar boundaries by 30 seconds (so bars are :00–:30, :30–:00 instead of :00–:01, etc.) and re-run the full pipeline without re-optimizing anything. If the depth-PF curve collapses or shifts dramatically, the structure is not robust to temporal aggregation and is likely overfitted to the bar clock.

**Attack 3: The null model is misspecified, and your stability is stable around a wrong center.**  
Your matched nulls likely preserve marginal statistics (firing rate, maybe variance) but not the **cross-sectional correlation structure** of the 171 raw features. Because the features are all built from the same price path, they share latent factors (volatility, trend, autocorrelation). A null that does not preserve this will make signals look significant when they are just loading on common factors. Build a **surrogate data** null: use the Fourier amplitude-adjusted Fourier transform (AAFT) or iterated AAFT to generate synthetic price paths with the same autocorrelation and cross-correlation spectra as your real data, but with the signal-return relationship destroyed. Run your entire pipeline (triple generation, filtering, depth floor, gates, cap) on 100 surrogate datasets. If any surrogate produces a "significant" book, your null is too permissive and your stability is illusory.

**Attack 4: The depth floor is a volatility filter wearing a consensus mask.**  
If your 171 features all load on realized volatility (many microstructure and volume features do), then the depth count is just a nonlinear measure of "how many things are excited right now." Volatile bars may have directional skew (e.g., intraday momentum during breakouts). Control for realized volatility at the bar level: stratify all depth-N events by their volatility quintile, and look at the PF *within* quintile. If the depth effect disappears inside a volatility stratum, you are not trading consensus; you are trading volatility regime, and your gates are just ad-hoc volatility conditioners.

**Attack 5: Meta-architecture multiple testing.**  
You have pre-registered the gates, but how many *families* of architecture did you try before arriving at "conjunction of three, depth floor, tiered gates, concurrency cap"? The effective search space is the product of: (a) feature engineering choices, (b) triple definitions, (c) depth thresholds, (d) gate logic families, (e) cap values, and (f) selection objectives. Even if each family is Bonferroni-corrected, the product is not. Apply **White’s Reality Check** or the **Romano-Wolf stepdown** to the final P&L series, where the "models" being compared are not trading rules but **entire system architectures** drawn from the plausible design space you actually explored. If the best architecture is not significant after this, the whole edifice is a data-mining artifact.

---

### Bottom line

Your empirical finding is real and important: there exists a subset of weak, mid-field signals that, when they co-occur, produce a near-deterministic readout. But that is not a feature-selection problem. It is a **latent-state estimation problem** where the state is encoded redundantly across a distributed population of detectors. The instability of deterministic selection is not a bug to fix; it is a theorem about the geometry of that encoding. The right response is not to hunt for a single stable subset, but to accept that any valid book must be a **diverse cover of the latent state space**, and to test the book as a collective object against a dependency-preserving null.

If I had to bet on what kills the system, it is not the 297 triples. It is the concurrency cap and the 1-minute bar boundary interacting to create a liquidity and timing fiction that does not survive contact with the market.
